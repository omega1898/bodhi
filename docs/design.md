**Build the game around one feeling: “I need to get back to you.”** Every separation, obstacle, sound, and reunion should reinforce that bond.

I would start with a small, complete iOS chapter that establishes the characters and mechanics, then expand that foundation into the PC game. The phone release should feel like a complete emotional experience.

**A possible story**

Working title: *Until We Find Home*.

Years after a catastrophe, you and your dog travel toward a place you remember as safe. The world is quiet: flooded neighborhoods, abandoned apartments, forests growing through roads. Your dog is your remaining connection to the life you lost.

You carry an old photograph of home. Your dog carries no understanding of the destination—only the certainty that wherever you go, they belong beside you.

Each chapter takes you through a place where another person once cared for someone: a hospital, a school, a railway station, an evacuation shelter. Environmental details reveal what happened without requiring constant dialogue.

**The central story tension:** you are chasing a place from the past, while your dog keeps pulling you toward life in the present.

The catastrophe needs consistent rules, but explaining it should occupy less attention than the relationship.

**How a level should work**

Give each level a shared destination and two connected routes. Separation should come from believable events: a collapsing walkway, a closing floodgate, a passage only the dog can enter.

| Part | What the player does | Emotional purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Together | Explore, play briefly, find the next destination | Establish safety and affection |
| Separation | An event divides the pair | Break that safety |
| Human section | Find a route and change the environment | Act to protect the dog |
| Dog section | Follow scent, navigate tight spaces, locate the human | Experience attachment from the dog’s perspective |
| Near contact | Hear a bark, glimpse movement, meet across a barrier | Renew hope |
| Final obstacle | Complete actions on both routes | Earn the reunion |
| Reunion | Restore physical contact and shared movement | Release tension |
| Quiet aftermath | Rest, inspect discoveries, continue together | Let the emotion settle |

**Make actions on one route help the other.** The human lowers a floating platform; the dog crosses it. The dog discovers a way through a building and barks from a window; the human changes direction. Players should understand that both characters are working toward each other.

Avoid switching characters on a fixed timer. Switch when the player has a clear unanswered question: *Did the dog make it across? Who is behind that door? Can they hear me?* Resolve enough uncertainty to maintain trust, then continue.

**An example first chapter: “The Other Side”**

You and your dog reach a flooded railway station before nightfall. Beyond it is a maintenance cabin where you can shelter.

Start with several minutes together. Let the player learn the dog’s habits: checking back at doorways, responding to a whistle, leaning into a hand when frightened.

A weakened walkway collapses. The dog lands on a lower platform; you remain above.

As the human, you restore power to a maintenance gate. Through the station, you hear your dog bark. You can whistle back.

The perspective changes to the dog. The whistle establishes direction, but water and debris block the direct route. The dog follows your scent through luggage rooms, loses it near rushing water, then finds a familiar glove caught on a railing.

Later, you meet on opposite sides of a fence. You can briefly touch the dog through it, but the gate is jammed. Now the player knows both are safe—and desperately wants to finish crossing.

The final actions open a shared route to the cabin. At the reunion, give the player control over approaching, kneeling, and making contact. Let the dog’s animation finish before presenting any objective.

End with a quiet scene: rain outside, the dog asleep against your leg, the glove drying beside the fire.

That is enough material for a strong first playable chapter.

**Make the dog feel like a dog**

The two characters need different ways of understanding the world.

| Human | Dog |
|---|---|
| Reads signs and maps | Recognizes familiar scents |
| Operates machinery and tools | Fits through narrow routes |
| Understands the destination | Recognizes people, routines, and immediate danger |
| Plans around structures | Notices movement, sounds, and animal behavior |
| Calls or whistles | Barks, scratches, nudges, and searches |

For scent, use a restrained visual effect that appears when the player actively sniffs. Combine it with nose movement, breathing, ear position, and sound. Avoid filling every scene with glowing trails.

The dog should occasionally hesitate, investigate, or seek reassurance. Those behaviors create personality, but controls must remain responsive. A player should understand when hesitation is part of the scene.

**The emotional design needs restraint**

Constant danger eventually becomes exhausting. Alternate tension with ordinary affection: sharing food, drying muddy paws, finding a ball, waiting while the dog investigates something.

Establish recognizable behaviors, then change them with context. Early on, your dog confidently runs ahead and checks back. After a frightening separation, they stay close for a while. That small change can communicate more than a speech.

Also, **do not make the dog’s death your default emotional payoff.** Your premise can deliver powerful emotion through survival, dependence, aging, trust, and reunion. Decide early what emotional promise the ending makes, then build toward it.

**Personalizing the human and dog**

This is a major feature, so give it its own production plan.

For the first version, use guided character creation:

- **Human:** skin tone, face presets, hair, body presets, clothing, name.
- **Dog:** body type, coat length, colors, markings, ears, tail, name.
- **Bond details:** collar, nickname, favorite toy, familiar command.

Allow reference photos beside the customization controls. Automatic photo reconstruction can come later; it creates substantial modeling, animation, privacy, and quality challenges.

The hardest issue is dog anatomy. A dachshund and a German shepherd cannot convincingly share every animation and fit through identical spaces. Start with a few supported body families and set clear size limits. Design passages, camera positions, and reunion animations around those limits.

**Prioritize recognizability through markings and behavior.** A familiar white paw, floppy ear, collar, and head tilt can carry considerable emotional weight.

**How iOS and PC should connect**

For this design, I would make the iOS release a short prologue with its own satisfying ending. It introduces the pair, demonstrates one separation and reunion, and ends as the larger journey begins.

Keep these foundations consistent across platforms:

- Character identity and customization data.
- Story and world rules.
- Core interaction logic.
- The dog’s personality and relationship behaviors.

Build separate visual and control treatments for each platform. PC can support denser environments, richer lighting, more detailed fur, and more elaborate effects. Mobile needs scenes, materials, and effects designed within a much tighter performance budget.

**Choose the engine after testing your hardest scene on an actual iPhone.** Do not commit based only on attractive PC screenshots. The important test is a scene containing the customized dog, fur, water, lighting, animation, and both characters together.

Your first prototype can use simple placeholder graphics. High visual quality becomes valuable once movement, switching, and attachment already work.

**What to build first**

Build one **10–15 minute chapter** with:

- One human and one dog preset.
- One compact location.
- One separation.
- Two perspective changes.
- One action by each character that helps the other.
- One meaningful reunion.
- One quiet ending.

Exclude combat, open-world travel, crafting, and full photo-based customization from that first build. Each adds work before you have answered the central question.

Then watch people play without coaching them. Ask:

1. When did you first care about the dog?
2. When separated, did you want to reunite or simply finish the puzzle?
3. Did playing as the dog feel distinct?
4. Was any uncertainty confusing rather than emotional?
5. Did the reunion feel earned?

**Your next action is to design that railway-station chapter on paper:** draw both routes, mark where the pair can hear or see each other, and identify exactly how each character helps the other. That becomes the blueprint for the first playable game.
